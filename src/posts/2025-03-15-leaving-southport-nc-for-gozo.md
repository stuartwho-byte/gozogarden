In 1975 I moved from London to Westchester County, New York, and spent the next several decades building a career in IT. I retired in 2009. My wife was already dying of cancer by then — we knew what was coming — and I lost her in 2010 — that's a chapter I'll leave closed here, but it's part of how this story starts. In 2011, I moved south to Southport, North Carolina, and settled into St. James Plantation: golf courses, tennis courts, swimming pools, the whole polished, brochure version of the American retirement dream. I met Sabina a few years later — she'd already been living in North Carolina long before I got there — and we married in 2016. Sabina is German — proud of it, and rightly so — and had already been living in the U.S. for thirty years by the time we met. Between the two of us, we're both dual citizens, which matters more to this story than it might sound.

The house I'd bought in St. James Plantation came with about half an acre attached to it, and "garden" was a generous word for what was actually there when I moved in — overgrown, tired, more suggestion than substance. Over the thirteen years I lived there, Sabina and I turned it into something else entirely. Clematis climbing where there'd been nothing. Four or five David Austin roses doing what David Austin roses do when they're happy. Oak leaf hydrangeas, and their more flamboyant multicolored cousins, filling out the beds. Grasses moving in the wind. And right in the middle of it all, a fir tree so enormous we'd named it — Rockefeller, after the tree, obviously — that ended up anchoring the whole space, the way one good piece of furniture can anchor a room.

![Clematis and roses climbing the brick wall](/images/leaving-southport-clematis-roses.jpg)

People who came to sit out there told us it felt like a private resort — inside a community that was already selling exactly that. I'll take the compliment anyway. Years of weekends, of trial and error, of things that thrived and things that didn't, builds a garden like that. It doesn't happen by accident, and it doesn't happen fast.

![Hydrangeas and black-eyed susans by the patio](/images/leaving-southport-hydrangeas.jpg)

And then, somewhere around year thirteen, we got itchy feet.

I don't think either of us could tell you the exact moment it started. It's more that after thirteen years in one place, you start eyeing the map again — not because anything's wrong, but because you built this life once and some part of you wonders if you could build another one somewhere else. We started talking about where "somewhere else" might actually be, and I came to the conversation with exactly two non-negotiables. First: they had to speak English. Not as a courtesy — as a necessity. I wanted to be able to stand in the driveway with a plumber and actually understand what he was telling me was wrong, without a translation app doing the work. Second: it had to be warm. Decades of New York winters and a handful of Carolina ones felt like enough for one lifetime.

That's a short list, but it turns out it doesn't rule out much of the English-speaking world. I handed Sabina those two criteria, and the next morning she came back with Malta. A little more digging narrowed it further, to Gozo — the quieter island next door, the one people describe as what Malta was fifty years ago. Friends back home still talk about how much courage it must have taken to just pick up and move to a Mediterranean island. Honestly, it wasn't courage so much as paperwork — with dual citizenship already sorted on both sides, moving into the EU was never the leap from the outside it looked like. We flew out for a month, not to vacation, but to actually scope it out: walking properties, getting a feel for towns, trying to picture ourselves there in a way that photos online never quite let you do.

That's how we found the house in St. Lawrenz — a rental this time, not a purchase. It had a walled garden, which mattered to me more than I expected it to, given I'd just spent thirteen years building one from scratch on the other side of the Atlantic, plus a pool and more space in the house itself than we'd had in Southport. We stood in that garden and it was obvious. Not a maybe. A dream, really, is the only honest word for it.

We signed the lease in April 2024, while the place was still mid-refurbishment from the previous tenant — new to us, but not exactly move-in ready yet. Back in North Carolina, we had the harder conversation: rent our own house out, or sell it outright. We landed on selling. Dealing with tenants and repairs from an ocean away wasn't the retirement we were picturing. We wanted a clean break, not a second property to manage remotely. So we found a buyer, closed it out, and started counting down.

Almost a year passed between signing the lease and actually landing. On March 15th, 2025, we got on a United flight out of the States to Frankfurt, connected through to Malta's main airport, and finally made the short hop over to Gozo. Rockefeller stayed behind, obviously — you can't exactly check a sixty-foot fir as luggage — along with the roses and the hydrangeas and thirteen years of knowing exactly which corner of that garden did what in which season.

Honestly, the garden itself wasn't something we spent that year thinking hard about. It was there, it was walled and pretty, it was part of what made the property feel right — but it stayed in the background while the lease, the sale, and the logistics of moving an entire life across an ocean took up all the oxygen. It wasn't until we actually arrived in March 2025 and started living in the space that I really looked at it properly. And what I found, once I did, was a completely different story — beautiful in its own way, but working against a completely different set of rules than anything my years of New York and North Carolina gardening had taught me. That's where the next entry picks up: what we actually found when we started digging.
