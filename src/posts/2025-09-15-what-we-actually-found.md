When we signed the lease, the house was mid-renovation — a new bathroom going in upstairs, the landlady walking us through everything else she planned to fix before we arrived. We were, in hindsight, naive about it. We didn't look nearly as closely as we should have.

What we found when we got here was disappointing. The garden had gone completely and utterly to weeds — the beds, the containers, all of it — with some genuinely stubborn ones pushing up through the cracks in the wall itself. Before anything else registered, that was the first thing you saw: just ugliness, wall to wall.

![Empty stone planter boxes along the wall, choked with weeds](/images/what-we-found-empty-planters.jpg)

The house wasn't much better. Sabine is Prussian German, and particular about her environment in the way that word implies — clean means clean. Whatever deep-cleaning company the landlady had used before we moved in didn't come close to her bar. We went two weeks without hot water in the kitchen. Doors didn't close properly. The furniture was old, ratty, and dusty. There were a few moments, early on, where we just looked at each other — the unspoken "what have we done" hanging in the room. We laughed it off each time. This was a decision we'd made, and we were going to make it work.

With the house in that state, the garden dropped to second place. We were too busy figuring out how to get food, get water, work the kitchen, and generally function day to day to give it much attention.

The landlady gave us the name of her own landscaper and said he'd be in touch. He turned up, eventually, on what we've since learned to call Gozo time — two or three weeks after the first contact. He walked the garden, said yes, he could help, shouldn't cost too much, not a problem, send some ideas over. And then he simply didn't. I spent four or five weeks chasing him — WhatsApp messages, showing up at his nursery in town — before it became obvious he had no real interest in the job.

So I went looking elsewhere. A couple of landscapers came round, quoted a few thousand euros, and it was clear within minutes of talking to them what they actually meant by that: marigolds, a row of pots, a layer of pine bark mulch, done. Not remotely what we wanted.

![An unkempt bed by the house, weeds around the AC unit and olive tree](/images/what-we-found-overgrown-bed.jpg)

One did something different. He responded quickly, walked the garden with us, and actually started painting a picture of what it could become. We told him what we were after — privacy from the neighbors along the south wall, color on the north wall by the pool, a proper garden in the back — and he came back with a written proposal. He had us come into his nursery and walked us through every plant on the list. What did I know at that point? Not much. I asked him directly about improving the soil, and he said yes, absolutely, that would be part of it.

The first thing that happened after we signed was genuinely good: a couple of guys spent three days clearing out the olive trees, and it looked great.

![A worker clearing brush during the three-day olive tree cleanup](/images/what-we-found-clearing-olive-trees.jpg)

The rest of the garden, though, still had the old lighting fixtures installed by whoever had it before us — the kind of very bright, very cold light that would have worked well in a German POW camp, wired straight into the mains, clearly a couple of decades old. No great loss, losing those.

When the crew came to improve the soil, they brought a small rotor tiller — and promptly hit the old lighting wires running through the beds, taking out the electrics in the process.

![Tilling a bare bed by hand ahead of planting](/images/what-we-found-tilling.jpg)

That cost a quick call to the landlady and a scramble to sort out. We agreed solar lights would come later.

Once the tilling was done, they worked in what they called compost. It was peat moss! I'm not a fan, and It didn't look like nearly enough to me, but again — what did I know? Then came the installation itself: a couple of hundred plants, delivered and popped straight into the ground, root systems never broken up, not touched. We were told to hand-water everything twice a day until the irrigation system went in, which didn't happen until September. All of it — the clearing, the tilling, the planting — happened that July.

And it looked good. Genuinely pretty, for a while.

![New planters of bougainvillea and succulents by the wall](/images/what-we-found-new-planters.jpg)

Thin, but pretty. It was a new garden.

![The new bed along the wall, young plants and climbing bougainvillea](/images/what-we-found-new-bed.jpg)

It wasn't until the following February, March, and April that I started to understand what had actually happened underneath all of it — and that's a story that needs its own chapter.
