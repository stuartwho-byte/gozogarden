For the better part of a year, this garden has been killing plants in the same handful of spots for what looked, each time, like a different reason. Lavender that should shrug off Gozo summers. Lantana that failed twice in one bed and thrived two feet away. Clematis that went from healthy to collapsed in about a week. Cycads that just gave up. Every death got its own investigation, its own suspect, its own half-plausible verdict — sunburn, a bad batch of stock, root rot, simple bad luck.

It turns out they were all the same crime.

## The line everyone walked over

Every planting hole on this site, dug the ordinary way to the ordinary depth, runs into resistance at somewhere between five and seven inches. Most of us — myself included, forty years of gardening in New York and North Carolina behind me — read that as the bottom of the topsoil and just kept going, or backfilled with nice amended mix and called it done. What's actually down there is a genuine plastic clay layer, part of the local Blue Clay Formation, sitting under nearly the entire garden like a lid. Water hits it, has nowhere to go, and sits. Every plant with amended, well-drained soil in its planting hole and this clay six inches under it is effectively growing in a bathtub with the plug in.

That's the theory I'd been circling for months. This summer it stopped being a theory. A moisture meter, pushed into the ground at both the thriving and the struggling ends of the same lantana bed, told the story in numbers: dry and reasonable at the surface, saturated and resistant right where the clay starts. Three separate trouble spots, same signature, every time.

## Why not just fix it

The obvious fix — break through the clay so water can escape — doesn't work here, and it took a failed tool to prove it. My soil consultants (a two-person outfit, actual microbiologists and chemists, doing this as a side venture) brought a power auger to drill through it on a site visit. It failed. Quietly, without much comment in their report. But a machine built to bore through ground failing to bore through this ground is data, not an inconvenience — it's confirmation that this clay is dense and, critically, plastic: it deforms and reseals rather than fracturing and staying open. Auger a hole through it and it heals itself. Any drainage strategy that depends on breaking through the clay is fighting a material that closes back up behind you.

So the plan isn't to defeat the hardpan. It's to stop pretending it isn't there. New beds go up, not down — mounded, built from mineral bulk and compost on top of the existing grade, roots kept up out of the saturation zone entirely rather than planted down into a hole that becomes a sump. It's a less satisfying fix than "solve the drainage," but it's the one that actually respects what the site is doing.

## The iron finally makes sense too

Some of this summer's other threads turned out to connect to the same root cause, if less directly. The chlorotic marmalade bush and the hibiscus that won't hold a bloom have both been on a foliar iron spray that helps a little and never lasts, because it's EDTA-chelated — and EDTA effectively stops working above about pH 6.5, which is nowhere near where this soil sits (7.8–8.0, with the irrigation water itself coming in at 8.2). Switched to an EDDHA-chelated iron instead, which is the one chelate form that actually holds up in calcareous soil like this, applied as a root-zone drench rather than a spray. It's not a permanent fix — the irrigation water re-alkalizes the soil continuously, so this becomes a standing maintenance task, not a one-time correction — but it's the first iron treatment aimed at what the soil is actually doing instead of what iron treatments are usually aimed at.

The hibiscus had a second, unrelated problem hiding under the first — spider mites, confirmed with the simple white-paper test, which explains the bud drop that the iron treatment was never going to fix on its own. Two problems wearing one set of symptoms is apparently just how this garden operates.

## What's next

The soil people are coming back within the month to find out what's actually underneath the clay — rock, or more soil — which will settle whether a French drain is even physically possible here. Until then, the mound-bed approach starts regardless of what they find, because it's the right call either way. First one goes in as a pilot, no more waiting for a subsurface survey to greenlight the obvious fix.
